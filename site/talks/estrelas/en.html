Stars are born inside molecular clouds: gas so cold, 10 to 20 kelvin, that the hydrogen is in molecular form. Sir James Jeans · 1902

When does gravity beat heat?

The idea

A region of radius R and mass M collapses if its molecules, stirred by the cloud’s temperature T, are not fast enough to escape from it.

Then gravity beats the pressure that comes from thermal energy, and any disturbance makes the region fall in on itself.

It is the same escape-velocity sum that will come back in Part V — there, with light in place of the molecules.

Mean kinetic energy of a molecule of mass m, and its gravitational potential energy at the edge of the region:

Kavg = 32 kBT
UG = GMmR

The threshold for collapse is where the two are equal:

32 kBT = GMmR,   M = 4π3 ρR³

Solving for R:

R = 9 kBT8πGρm1/2
Jeans’s question: when does the gravity of a piece of cloud beat the thermal motion? The estimate is an escape-velocity one. Each molecule has kinetic energy three halves kT; if that is less than the potential energy GMm/R, it cannot escape and the piece collapses. Setting the two equal and using M = 4π/3 ρR³ gives a critical radius. The colder and denser the cloud, the smaller the radius — and the smaller the mass — that collapses on its own.

Jeans length and Jeans mass

The denser the cloud, the smaller the piece that collapses

In a more careful analysis, with pressure perturbations, Jeans found:

λJ = π kBTGρm1/2Jeans length
MJ = 4π3 λJ3 ρ = 4π5/23 kBTGm3/2 ρ−1/2Jeans mass
MJ ∝ T3/2 ρ−1/2

As the cloud collapses and gets denser, the mass that can collapse on its own decreases: the cloud breaks up into pieces — and each piece becomes a star. That is why stars are born in groups.

Jeans mass · H₂ gasdrag the controls
Jeans’s full calculation swaps the 9/8π for π and gives the Jeans length; the Jeans mass is the mass of a sphere of that size. The key point: M_J grows with temperature and falls with density, as ρ^(−1/2). In the plot, each curve is one temperature. A typical cloud, at 10 K and a thousand molecules per cm³, has a Jeans mass of about a hundred solar masses; in a dense core, with a million per cm³, it drops to a few solar masses. Below, the free-fall time: one to ten million years in the cloud, less in the dense cores.

Collapse and fragmentation

From the cloud to protostars

illustration · gravity between 900 lumps of gas

The cloud falls in on itself. The densest regions collapse fastest — the free-fall time goes as ρ−1/2 — and the collapse fragments the cloud into several cores.

Each fragment spins faster and faster as it shrinks, like a skater pulling in her arms: it forms a disc, with a protostar at its centre.

Illustration, not simulation: particles with softened gravity and a little thermal motion, in a plane. Real gas has pressure, turbulence, magnetic fields and radiation — full simulations in Krumholz, Klein & McKee, ApJ 656, 959 (2007).

This is an illustration of what the simulations show. Nine hundred lumps of gas pulling on one another, with a little random motion. The clumps that form first are the densest; when a clump gets compact enough it becomes a protostar — the bright dot — and starts swallowing the gas around it. Notice that you don’t get one star: you get several. Real simulations, like those of Krumholz, Klein and McKee, include radiation and show the same thing: fragmentation, discs and multiple systems.

The difference is temperature

What is the difference between a cloud and a star?

contraction→rising temperature→black body→visible light→a star is born!
A cloud, a protostar and a star are the same gas at different temperatures. As it contracts, the cloud turns gravitational energy into heat; half leaves as radiation, half heats the gas — that is the virial theorem. Hot gas glows as a black body. Once the surface passes about 3,000 K, the glow reaches the visible. In the middle, the HL Tauri disc seen by ALMA: the dark rings are probably planets forming. And when the centre passes 10 million kelvin, hydrogen starts to fuse: a star is born.

Black-body radiation · Planck 1900

The hotter the star, the bluer the peak and the far greater the brightness: doubling T multiplies the luminosity by 16.

The spectral classes O B A F G K M are a temperature scale: from over 30,000 K (blue) to under 3,700 K (red). The Sun is a G2.

“Almost”: the real spectrum has absorption lines from the elements in the atmosphere, and that is how we know what stars are made of.

black-body spectrum · colour of the stardrag the temperature
To a first approximation, the spectrum of a star is that of a black body at the surface temperature. The solid curve is Planck’s law per unit wavelength; the coloured band is the visible range. The circle in the corner is the colour the eye would see, computed by integrating the curve against the eye’s colour-matching functions. Notice there are no green stars: when the peak is in the green, the spectrum covers the whole visible range and the star looks white. Rigel, Sirius, the Sun, Betelgeuse: blue, white, yellowish white, reddish orange.

Hayashi · Henyey · 1961

From protostar to main sequence

  • The protostar starts as an invisible concentration of gas deep inside the cloud.
  • It contracts and heats up, hidden in a cocoon of dust: it only shows up in the infrared.
  • It blows the cocoon away and becomes visible, still shrinking: first almost straight down the diagram (the Hayashi track), then to the left (the Henyey track).
  • When the centre reaches ~10 million K, hydrogen starts to fuse: the star joins the main sequence and stops shrinking.

Schematic tracks, with key points roughly following Iben’s models (1965). The more massive the star, the faster: ~50 million years for 1 M☉, ~1 million for 5 M☉.

The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram: luminosity against temperature, with temperature increasing to the left. The protostar starts cool and large, on the right; while it is inside its dust cocoon we only see it in the infrared. It moves down almost vertically — it is shrinking at nearly constant temperature, fully convective: the Hayashi track. Then it turns left, heating up at nearly constant luminosity: the Henyey track. When hydrogen ignites at the centre, it settles on the main sequence. A star like the Sun takes about 50 million years over this; one of five solar masses, a million.

What is a star’s life like?

Brightness, stability… and peace?

Brightness.

Stability.

Peace.

Not peace. At the centre of every star, non-stop, the reaction of a hydrogen bomb is going on.

What is a star’s life like? Seen from here: brightness, stability — the Sun has shone almost unchanged for four and a half billion years. Peace? No. What keeps that brightness steady is a continuous nuclear explosion, held in by the star’s own gravity.

Fusion in the Sun runs on the proton–proton chain: four protons become a helium nucleus, two positrons, two neutrinos and 26.7 MeV. The difference from a bomb is that gravity holds the explosion in. The numbers: the Sun’s luminosity, 3.8×10²⁶ watts, divided by c² gives 4.3 million tonnes of mass turning into energy every second; since only 0.7% of the mass becomes energy, that is 610 million tonnes of hydrogen per second. In bombs: Ivy Mike released 4×10¹⁶ joules; the Sun releases nine billion of those every second. And every reaction gives off neutrinos: 65 billion per square centimetre per second are passing through you right now.

Hydrostatic equilibrium

A violent balance

dPdr = − G m(r) ρ(r)r²in every layer: pressure against weight

In every layer, the weight of everything above is held up by the difference in pressure. If the star shrinks, the centre heats up, fusion speeds up and the pressure pushes back; if it swells, it cools and fusion slows down. A thermostat.

temperature15.7 million K

Without pressure, the Sun would collapse in ~30 minutes — the free-fall time at its mean density.

Hydrostatic equilibrium: in every layer of the star, gravity pulls inwards — the violet arrows — and the pressure difference pushes outwards — the amber arrows. In the animation the star gets disturbed: compressed, pressure wins and it bounces back; expanded, gravity wins and it comes back. It oscillates around equilibrium, and fusion works as a thermostat. The pressure at the centre of the Sun is 250 billion atmospheres. And the balance is violent in the literal sense: if the pressure switched off, the Sun would fall in on itself in half an hour.

Mass, luminosity and lifetime

More mass, shorter life

main sequencedrag the mass
L ∝ M3.5mass–luminosity relation (2 to 55 M☉)
t ≈ 1010 years · M/M☉L/L☉ ∝ M−2.5fuel ÷ burning rate

A 10 M☉ star has ten times more fuel, but shines thousands of times brighter: it lives ~30 million years. The Sun, ~10 billion. A red dwarf of 0.2 M☉, trillions — longer than the age of the Universe.

Every low-mass star ever born is still alive.

Mass decides everything. Luminosity grows as a high power of mass — close to M to the 3.5 — because a heavier star needs more pressure at the centre, hence a higher temperature, hence much faster fusion. The fuel only grows as M. The lifetime, fuel divided by burning rate, falls as M to the −2.5. The amber curve is the luminosity, the turquoise one the lifetime. Massive stars live tens of millions of years; red dwarfs, trillions — no red dwarf has died yet.

The H–R diagram

Temperature (or colour) on the horizontal axis, increasing to the left; luminosity on the vertical axis.

  • Main sequence: the diagonal, where stars spend 90% of their lives burning hydrogen in the core.
  • Giants and supergiants: cool and huge, top right.
  • White dwarfs: hot and small, bottom left.

When the star runs out of hydrogen in its core, little energy is left to support the weight of the layers above — 90% of the star.

The H–R diagram is the map of the stars. Each dot is a star, with temperature on the horizontal axis — reversed, hot on the left — and luminosity on the vertical. Most of them lie on the diagonal, the main sequence. Stars do not travel along the main sequence: each one sits at a point set by its mass while it burns hydrogen in the core. What happens when that hydrogen runs out is the subject of the next slide.

When the core runs out of hydrogen

Three things at once

evolution on the H–R diagram · schematic

1 · Gravity starts to win

The helium core contracts, and the contraction relights hydrogen burning in a shell around it.

2 · The core heats up

Until another reaction ignites: ⁴He → ¹²C (triple-alpha, at 100 million K); in massive stars, ¹²C → ¹⁶O → … → ⁵⁶Fe, in shells like those of an onion.

3 · The outer layers swell

The energy pushes the envelope out: the star grows and cools — a red giant. Above ~8 M☉, a red supergiant.

When the core runs out of hydrogen, three things happen together. The core, now helium, loses its source of pressure and contracts; the contraction heats the region around it and hydrogen starts burning in a shell. The core heats up to 100 million kelvin and ignites the fusion of helium into carbon. And the extra energy pushes on the envelope, which swells and cools: the star becomes a red giant — the Sun will swallow Mercury and Venus. With 15 solar masses the story is faster and goes further: carbon, oxygen, neon, silicon, all the way to iron, in shells. Iron is the end of the line: fusing iron uses up energy instead of releasing it.

The two paths. Stars of up to about eight solar masses become red giants, blow off their envelope as a planetary nebula and leave the core exposed: a white dwarf, the size of the Earth. Above eight, they become supergiants — Betelgeuse is one — and end in a core-collapse supernova; what is left is a neutron star or, for the most massive ones, a black hole. The mass limits are approximate and depend on composition and mass loss. The images of the remnants are real: the planetary nebula, Cassiopeia A, the Crab and the shadow of M87*.

Gravitational collapse

When the fuel runs out

Without nuclear reactions, the pressure they kept up is gone: gravity wins and the star’s matter goes into free fall.

How long does it take?

tff = 3π32 G ρ1/2

At the Sun’s mean density, ~30 minutes. For the iron core of a massive star, ~0.1 s.

For some stars there is still a way out: degeneracy pressure.

When the fuel finally runs out, there is nothing left to hold things up. The matter falls freely, and the timescale is the free-fall time: half an hour for something at the Sun’s mean density, a tenth of a second for the iron core of a massive star. But for some stars there is still a rescue, and it comes not from heat but from quantum mechanics. That is Part III.

The two compact corpses, to scale. A white dwarf has the mass of the Sun in the size of the Earth: Sirius B, the companion of Sirius, is a little smaller than the Earth and has one solar mass. A teaspoonful of its material weighs about five tonnes. A neutron star packs one and a half solar masses into a radius of twelve kilometres — it would fit inside a city. The teaspoonful weighs half a billion tonnes. The question is: what holds this up, if there is no more fusion?

Quantum mechanics · 1925 – 1927

Heisenberg and Pauli to the rescue

Uncertainty principle · 1927

Δp Δx ≥ ħ2

Confining a particle to a small region forces its momentum to be large.

Exclusion principle · 1925

Two identical fermions — electrons, protons, neutrons — cannot occupy the same quantum state.

Together: squeezing fermions costs energy, even at zero temperature.

The star is saved by two principles of quantum mechanics. Heisenberg: the more confined a particle is, the larger the uncertainty in its momentum — and so its typical momentum. Pauli: two identical fermions cannot occupy the same state. Together, they say that compressing a gas of electrons or neutrons forces the particles to have ever larger momenta, even with no temperature at all. That is a pressure — degeneracy pressure.

Pauli exclusion principle

Why fermions push back

electrons in a box · two per levelsqueeze the box

Each energy level holds two electrons: one with spin up, the other spin down. The next ones are forced to go higher.

In a box of width Δx, the levels are spaced by Δp ≈ ħ/Δx. With N electrons, the last one has momentum N/2 · Δp: if Ne = 6, the maximum momentum is 3Δp.

Squeezing the box pushes the levels apart: the electrons gain momentum with no need for heat. That is degeneracy pressure.

The animation fills a box with electrons. Each level takes two, with opposite spins; the third electron already has to go to the level above, and so on. With six electrons, the most energetic one has a momentum of three times Δp. Now squeeze the box: Δp grows as 1/Δx, all the levels go up, and the total energy grows even at zero temperature. That energy is what resists compression. In white dwarfs it is the electrons that do this; in neutron stars, the neutrons.

Curve: Nauenberg’s approximation (1972) for an electron gas at any degree of relativity. Points: Sirius B, Procyon B and 40 Eridani B. The more mass, the smaller the white dwarf — and at MCh the radius goes to zero.

Chandrasekhar did the calculation on the ship to Cambridge, at 19. If the electrons become relativistic, degeneracy pressure grows more slowly with density and, above a certain mass, it can no longer hold: 1.44 solar masses for carbon and oxygen. In the animation, a white dwarf gains mass from a companion. It gets smaller — heavier white dwarfs are smaller — and the radius goes to zero at the limit. The three points are measured white dwarfs. Go past M_Ch, and it collapses.

M > MCh

Collapse! Next stop: neutron stars

At the end of a massive star’s life, the iron core passes 1.4 M☉ and the electrons can no longer hold it up. In less than a second it falls from ~3,000 km to ~30 km, the electrons are captured by the protons, turning them into neutrons — and the rest of the star is blown off in a supernova. A neutron star is left at the centre.

core collapsesupernovaremnantpulsar + nebula

Artist’s animation · NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center Conceptual Image Lab (animation: Jonathan North) · SVS 20413 · public domain. A white dwarf that crosses the limit by accreting matter also explodes — a type Ia supernova — but leaves nothing behind.

When the iron core of a massive star passes the Chandrasekhar limit, the electrons cannot hold it and it collapses in free fall, in tenths of a second. The electrons are pushed into the protons — p + e → n + ν — and the core becomes a ball of neutrons; 99% of the energy leaves as neutrinos. The rest of the star is thrown outwards: a supernova. The NASA animation shows what you would see from outside: the flash, the expanding remnant and, at the centre, the pulsar with its nebula. One detail: a lone white dwarf that crosses the limit by gaining mass does not become a neutron star — it detonates completely, in a type Ia supernova.

In its place is the Crab Nebula, still expanding at 1,500 km/s, and at its centre a neutron star spinning 30 times a second. Cambridge · 28 November 1967

The first pulsar: “LGM-1”

Bell noticed a bit of “scruff” that kept coming back on the radio telescope’s paper chart: pulses every 1.337 s, as regular as clockwork. Nickname: LGM-1, for Little Green Men. It was a spinning neutron star.

Jocelyn Bell, Antony Hewish’s PhD student in Cambridge, was going through kilometres of paper chart from a radio telescope she had helped to build herself. In August 1967 she noticed a little bit of “scruff” that always came back at the same sidereal time. On 28 November she managed to resolve the pulses: one every 1.337 seconds, with clockwork precision. They joked that it was a signal from little green men, LGM-1. Today it is called PSR B1919+21. The 1974 Nobel Prize went to Hewish and Ryle, and not to her; in 2018 she won the Breakthrough Prize and donated the three million dollars to fund scholarships for students under-represented in physics.

What we measure from a pulsar is its period and how much it grows each second, Ṗ. Assuming a magnetic dipole spinning in vacuum, that gives the characteristic age, the magnetic field and the rotational energy lost. In the P–Ṗ diagram, each dot is a pulsar from the ATNF catalogue. 27 December 2004 · SGR 1806−20

A giant flare released ~10⁴⁶ erg in 0.2 s — more than the Sun emits in 100,000 years. At its peak, ~7×10⁴⁶ erg/s: some 20 trillion suns. It disturbed the Earth’s ionosphere from ~28,000 light-years away.

Magnetars were discovered as two different things: sources that repeatedly gave off bursts of soft gamma rays, the SGRs, and X-ray pulsars with anomalous brightness, the AXPs. They are the same class. They spin slowly, yet shine in X-rays more than all the rotational energy they lose — so rotation cannot be the source. The standard model says the source is the decay of magnetic fields of 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ gauss. In 2004, SGR 1806−20 let off a giant flare that, for two tenths of a second, shone like some 20 trillion suns (Hurley et al. and Palmer et al., Nature 2005, with the distance revised to 8.7 kpc). A correction to the 2018 version of this talk: it said there that the Sun produces 10¹³ erg over its whole life and an SGR 10³⁰ erg/s; the right values are ~10⁵¹ erg and ~10³²–10³⁵ erg/s in quiescence, with giant flares reaching ~10⁴⁷ erg/s. The order-of-magnitude comparison is still impressive.

Mass and radius with real nuclear physics

A neutron star is not a ball of free neutrons: the pressure comes from the nuclear force, described by relativistic mean-field models.

  • With these curves, M, R and the moment of inertia I are no longer canonical values: they are computed.

Two checks for SGR J1745−2900. The efficiency stays below one for masses above one solar mass, in all three equations of state: rotation accounts for the brightness. I · the antipodal spot never appears, and the primary is seen all the time. II · the primary always, and the antipodal for part of the time. III · the primary disappears for a fraction of the period. IV · both are seen all the time.

For each pair of angles i and j, the code computes the light curve and the pulsed fraction, and builds the map. The ingredients come from different places: mass and radius from the equation of state; the size and temperature of the spots from the spectral fit; the separation between the spots and the viewing angles from the pulsed fraction and the shape of the pulse. The final collapse

Nothing can stop the collapse

No known force can hold the star up.

And what if there is some physics we don’t know yet, strong enough to halt the collapse?

It makes no difference: once the event horizon forms, it is too late. Inside, every future points to the centre.

No known force can hold it. And the natural question: what if there is some new, stronger physics? The general-relativity argument is that it does not matter: as soon as the star is smaller than its own Schwarzschild radius, a horizon forms, and inside it stopping is as impossible as stopping time. We will see why over the course of this part. But first, the history.

The idea is older than relativity. John Michell, in 1783, and Laplace, in 1796, realised that, by Newton’s mechanics, a compact enough object would have an escape velocity greater than the speed of light. The relativistic version starts with Schwarzschild, in 1916. Eddington resisted the idea; Oppenheimer and Snyder calculated the collapse in 1939; Kerr found the rotating solution in 1963. The name “black hole” was popularised by Wheeler from 1967 onwards — in the 2018 version of this talk I had put 1950, which is wrong. And in 2019 came the first image.

1796 · Pierre-Simon Laplace

A dark star

By Newton’s physics, light could not escape from a sufficiently massive and compact object.

mv²2 − GMmR = 0  ⇒  vesc = 2GMR1/2initial energy = final energy

On Earth, vesc = 11.18 km/s. At what r would the escape velocity equal the speed of light?

r = 2GMc²

Earth: 8.9 mm. Sun: 2.95 km.

squeeze the Earthfixed mass
Laplace, in 1796, with Newton’s physics: the escape velocity grows when the same object gets smaller. Setting the kinetic energy equal to the potential energy gives v_esc = the square root of 2GM/R, 11.18 km/s on Earth. For that to be the speed of light, the radius has to be 2GM/c²: the whole Earth squeezed into a 9-millimetre marble, the Sun into 3 km. By coincidence, it is exactly the radius general relativity will give for the horizon — but for different reasons: in Newton, light would rise and fall back, like a ball; in GR it does not even get out.

December 1915 · the Russian front

A month after Einstein

Barely a month after Einstein presented General Relativity (25 November 1915), Schwarzschild — serving in the artillery on the Russian front — found an exact analytical solution of the equations.

“I have read your paper with the utmost interest. I had not expected that one could formulate the exact solution of the problem in such a simple way. I liked very much your mathematical treatment of the subject.”
Einstein to Schwarzschild, 9 January 1916

Schwarzschild died in May 1916, of an illness he contracted at the front.

Einstein presented the field equations on 25 November 1915. Schwarzschild, an astronomer aged 42, was on the Russian front as an artillery lieutenant; he read the paper and in December sent off the exact solution for the exterior of a spherical star. Einstein replied delighted — he himself had only used approximations. Schwarzschild died five months later, of pemphigus, a skin disease he caught at the front.

Spherical, uncharged, non-rotating

The first exact solution was already a black hole

What neither of them knew

The solution was complete: besides the exterior of a star, it described a spherical, neutral, non-rotating black hole — the Schwarzschild black hole.

Until then

Einstein had only looked for approximate solutions — the post-Newtonian formalism — and used them to explain the bending of light by the Sun and the precession of Mercury.

It took some 50 years for the community to accept that such a thing really existed.

What neither of them knew: Schwarzschild’s solution does not only describe the outside of a star. If the star is smaller than the Schwarzschild radius, there is no star at all — there is a black hole. The first exact solution of general relativity was already a black hole. Einstein worked with approximations, which were enough for Mercury and for light bending. It took half a century for the idea to be taken seriously — and another half for the photo.

Royal Astronomical Society · 11 January 1935

Eddington versus Chandrasekhar

One of the first to understand General Relativity, Eddington saw it at once: if Chandrasekhar was right about the mass limit, massive stars would inevitably form black holes.

“I think there should be a law of Nature to prevent a star from behaving in this absurd way!”
Arthur Eddington, Royal Astronomical Society, 11 January 1935

Chandrasekhar would later regret that it took almost thirty years for the significance of his result to be recognised.

K. C. Wali, Chandra: a biography of S. Chandrasekhar (1991).

In 1935, Chandrasekhar, aged 24, presented the mass limit at the Royal Astronomical Society. Eddington, the greatest authority in astrophysics at the time, spoke straight after him and ridiculed the result: there should be a law of Nature to stop a star from behaving in such an absurd way. He had understood the consequence perfectly — endless collapse — and refused to accept it. Chandrasekhar later lamented that it took almost thirty years for the result to be taken seriously. He won the Nobel Prize in 1983. (The 2018 version had a verbatim quote from him; since I could not check the exact source, a paraphrase remains, with Wali’s biography as the reference. Eddington’s sentence is in the report of the meeting, The Observatory 58, 38, 1935.)

1939 · 1963 · 1967

The collapse, the spin and the name

Three steps. Oppenheimer and his student Hartland Snyder, in 1939, calculated the collapse of a cloud of dust all the way to the end, and saw that, for someone far away, the star seems to freeze at the horizon. The same year, with Volkoff, Oppenheimer calculated the maximum mass of a neutron star. In 1963 Roy Kerr, a New Zealander, found the solution for a rotating black hole. And in 1967 Wheeler began to use “black hole”, and it stuck — the term had already appeared in the press in 1964. In French, “trou noir” had an obscene connotation, and there was resistance for a while.

The Schwarzschild metric

Three slices of the same formula

Δs² = −(1 − 2GMc²r)(cΔt)² + (1 − 2GMc²r)−1(Δr)² + r²[(Δθ)² + sin²θ (Δφ)²]M = mass of the central object

Δt = Δθ = Δφ = 0

Radial distances measured with a ruler: larger than Δr.

Δr = Δθ = Δφ = 0

Δτ = (1 − 2GM/c²r)1/2 Δt

The time on a clock at rest: it runs slower than one far away.

Δt = Δr = 0

ΔS = r [(Δθ)² + sin²θ (Δφ)²]1/2

The angular part is the familiar one: the distance between two points on the Earth’s surface (θ = 90° − latitude).

The Schwarzschild metric tells us how to measure intervals near a mass. It has three pieces, and each slice isolates one of them. Holding time and angle fixed, the radial part is left: the distance a ruler measures, larger than the coordinate difference. Holding position fixed, the time part is left: the time a clock at rest shows, less than the time far away. And fixing t and r, what is left is the geometry of an ordinary sphere — the same formula that gives the distance between two cities on Earth. That is why r has a simple meaning: the circumference divided by 2π.

Proper distance

The ruler in the well

  • r is the coordinate of an observer at infinity: the circumference divided by 2π.
  • Δrp is the distance a local observer measures with a ruler.
  • Distances are larger than they would be in flat space: between the spheres of circumference 2πr₁ and 2πr₂ there is more than r₂ − r₁.
  • The factor (1 − rs/r)−1/2 diverges at rs, but the distance to the horizon is finite: from 3rs down to rs it is 3.6 rs, not 2.

On the left, Flamm’s paraboloid: the equatorial slice (constant t) drawn as a surface with the same geometry.

What does it mean for radial distance to be larger? Flamm’s paraboloid is the slice of space drawn as a surface with the same geometry. From three Schwarzschild radii down to the horizon it is 3.6 r_s.

The Schwarzschild radius

Where the factor (1 − 2GM/c²r) in the metric vanishes. There is nothing there — no surface, no wall: it is a causal boundary. What goes in does not come out.

The Schwarzschild radius is proportional to mass: 2.95 km for every solar mass. The Earth would have to fit into 9 mm; a neutron star’s radius is only three times its Schwarzschild radius — that is why gravity there is so relativistic. The black hole at the centre of the Galaxy has a horizon of 18 solar radii; the one in M87, larger than Pluto’s orbit. And the horizon is not a special place for someone falling in: there is no wall, just a boundary of causality.

Proper time

Time in the well

two identical clocks · one far away, one in the welllower the clock
Δτ = (1 − 2GMc²r)1/2 Δt
  • The clock deep in the well runs slower, as seen from far away — and it is no illusion: when it comes back up, it shows less time.
  • GPS: +45 μs/day from altitude, −7 μs/day from the satellites’ speed. Without the correction, an error of ~10 km a day.
  • At the surface of a neutron star: Δτ/Δt ≈ 0.8 — some 12 minutes lost every hour.
  • At the horizon, Δτ/Δt → 0.
Two identical clocks: one far from the mass, the other at rest at a distance r. The lower one runs slower, by the factor square root of 1 − r_s/r. This is measured every day: the clocks on GPS satellites run 45 microseconds a day fast because they are higher up, and 7 slow because of their speed. At the surface of a neutron star, a clock runs at 80% of the rate far away. And as the clock goes down to the horizon, its rate goes to zero.

What happens at the horizon?

Seen from afar, time freezes

a probe falls in and blinks every second of its own clockradial fall, Schwarzschild

An observer at infinity would see time freeze at RS. Does that mean light stops?

No! It means it is trapped. For photons Δs² = 0; if Δτ → 0, the spatial terms vanish too (Δr = Δθ = Δφ = 0): the photon does not leave that region.

Photons: our natural clocks

A wave of frequency f is a clock that ticks 1/Δt times per second:

f∞ = (1 − 2GM/c²r)1/2 flocal

Whoever falls in notices nothing special when crossing the horizon.

A probe falls in radially and flashes a light every second of its own clock. Far away, we receive the flashes ever further apart and ever redder: the light loses energy climbing out of the well and the probe’s clock runs slow, as seen from here. The image of the probe freezes and fades near the horizon — we never see it cross. But it does cross, in a finite time on its own clock, and feels nothing special. Light it emits once inside the horizon never arrives. Photons are natural clocks: gravitational redshift is the same time dilation as on the previous slide.

Beyond the horizon

Every future points to the centre

Matter must move inside the light cones.

  • Far away, the cone is the familiar one: you can go outwards or inwards.
  • Near the horizon, it tips inwards, but still leaves a way out.
  • At the horizon, the outer edge becomes vertical: “outgoing” light stands still.
  • Inside, both sides of the cone point to smaller r: moving towards the future means moving towards the centre.

Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates: t̃ = t + rs ln|r/rs − 1|; infalling light follows straight lines at 45°.

At every point in spacetime, the light cone marks out the possible futures: nothing travels faster than light. In coordinates that do not break down at the horizon, the Eddington–Finkelstein ones, the cones tip further inwards as we get closer. At the horizon the outer edge becomes vertical: a light ray pointed outwards stays put at r = r_s. Inside, the whole cone points inwards. There, r plays the role of time: going to the centre is as unavoidable as going to tomorrow. What happens in a black hole stays in a black hole.

Kerr geodesics · 1963

The black hole that spins

light rays in the equatorial planedrag the spin
  • The rotation drags the spacetime around it (the Lense–Thirring effect).
  • Light travelling with the rotation gets closer before being captured: the photon orbit goes from 1.5 RS down to 0.5 RS as a → M.
  • Against the rotation, the orbit moves out, up to 2 RS.
  • Capture becomes asymmetric: the shadow shifts and flattens on one side.

Kerr null geodesics in Boyer–Lindquist coordinates, integrated in Mino time.

Kerr’s solution, from 1963, describes a spinning black hole — and every real black hole should spin. The same beam of light is now dragged along by the rotation. Rays passing on the side that turns with them get closer before being captured; those passing against it are captured from farther out. The capture boundary becomes asymmetric, and so does the shadow. At maximum spin, the prograde photon orbit touches the horizon.

r = 0

The singularity

Δs² = −(1 − 2GMc²r)(cΔt)² + (1 − 2GMc²r)−1(Δr)² + r²[(Δθ)² + sin²θ (Δφ)²]   r = 0 → singularity

r = RS is not singular

The metric seems to blow up there, but that is just a bad choice of coordinates — like longitude at the North Pole. Whoever falls in crosses the horizon without noticing, and the curvature there is finite.

r = 0 is

The curvature diverges — the Kretschmann invariant goes as 48G²M²/c⁴r⁶ — and tidal forces stretch anything apart. General Relativity predicts its own limit: a quantum theory of gravity has to take over there, and we do not have one yet.

To close the physics: there are two places where something in the metric diverges. At the horizon, r = R_S, the divergence is in the coordinates, not in spacetime; in other coordinates, like the Eddington–Finkelstein ones of the cones, everything is smooth there. At r = 0 it is real: the curvature goes to infinity. That is the point where general relativity stops working, and where quantum mechanics and gravity would have to meet.

Recap

Three masses decide the end

MJ

Jeans

Above it, a cold cloud collapses, fragments and lights up stars. It shrinks as the cloud gets denser.

1.44 M☉

Chandrasekhar

Above it, degenerate electrons cannot hold: there is no white dwarf, and the iron core collapses.

~2.2 M☉

Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff

Above it, not even neutrons can hold: a horizon forms, and the star becomes a black hole.

The whole life of a star is gravity on one side and, on the other, heat, fusion and — in the end — quantum mechanics.

The talk in three numbers. The Jeans mass decides when a cloud turns into stars. During its life, a star holds off gravity with fusion. At the end, if the mass of what is left is below 1.44 solar masses, electron pressure holds: a white dwarf. Between that and about 2.2, neutron pressure and the nuclear force hold: a neutron star. Above that, nothing holds: a black hole. Thank you.
