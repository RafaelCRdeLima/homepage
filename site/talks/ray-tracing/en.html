The premise of the seminar. A neutron star is about twelve kilometres in radius and hundreds of parsecs away: we will never see it resolved. Everything we know comes from photons, and those photons were born deep in a gravitational well. The direct problem — given the star, predict the signal — is ray tracing. The inverse one is inference. And if we change the metric, the signal changes: the same observations become a test of gravity. That is the thread running through the three parts.

How it is measured. Two photographs of the same patch of sky: the Hyades cluster, in Taurus. One at night, in July, when the Sun is already far away; the other during the eclipse, with the Sun right in front. The panel alternates between them, like a blink comparator, and everything is at true scale, with no exaggeration. On the whole plate nothing moves: one arcsecond is a tenth of a pixel on this screen. But nobody measured by eye: the plate went to a machine with a micrometer, which is what the loupes show — six arcseconds of sky around a star, with the wires at the night position. The star image is about two arcseconds wide, from turbulence and the optics, and during the eclipse it jumps by almost half of that, always away from the Sun, the symbol on the rim of the loupe. Near the Sun it jumps more, far away less: the deflection falls as 1/b. On the plate of the 4-inch lens, with a 5.8-metre focal length, one arcsecond is 28 micrometres. The centre of a blob can be measured much better than its width, and the fit for scale and rotation uses all the stars together. Click a star to put it in a loupe. Switch to Newton: the jump drops by half. That was the difference Sobral had to tell apart, and it measured 1.98″.

The Schwarzschild metric is the laboratory for everything that follows. The three characteristic radii: horizon, photon sphere, ISCO.

What we need from the metric

Curvature does three things to a photon

1 · It bends the path

The photon follows a null geodesic. Near the surface of a neutron star the bending is tens of degrees: we see more than half of the star.

2 · It changes the energy

A photon emitted at r with local energy E reaches infinity with

E∞ = E √(1 − RS/r)gravitational redshift

3 · It delays

Different paths take different times (Shapiro delay): photons from each point of the star arrive tens of microseconds apart.

Ray tracing puts all three into a single calculation, pixel by pixel, for each instant of the rotation.

In a neutron star, R ≈ 2.5–3 RS: none of the three effects is a small correction.

And all three depend only on the metric outside the star — which is why a light curve carries information about gravity.

What the metric does to a photon, in three items. It bends the path: at the surface of a neutron star, a ray leaving at grazing incidence is deflected by almost 90 degrees, and that is why we see more than half of the surface. It changes the energy: the gravitational redshift, some 20 to 30% for a typical star. And it delays: light from different points travels along different paths. Ray tracing puts the three together. And since everything depends on the exterior metric, changing the theory of gravity changes the result — keep that in mind for Part III.

The metric depends on neither t nor φ: energy and angular momentum are conserved.

The mechanics of null geodesics. Since the metric changes neither with time nor with angle, there are two constants, E and L, and for light only their ratio matters: the impact parameter b. The radial equation becomes that of a particle with energy 1/b² in a potential V(r) = (1 − R_S/r)/r². On the left, that potential and the line 1/b²; where the line lies below the potential, the ray cannot get in — that is the turning point. On the right, the orbit with the same b. When 1/b² rises above the peak, at 1.5 R_S, the ray falls in. That is the photon sphere, and the critical b, 2.6 R_S, is the size of a black hole’s shadow.

Compactness

Where GR stops being a correction

the radius and the Schwarzschild radius of the same mass

Surface compared with infinity: z = (1 − rs/R)−1/2 − 1, g = (GM/R²)(1 − rs/R)−1/2 · deflection from the exact orbit integral (weak field: 2rs/R) · ISCO, photon sphere and horizon: a 1.4 M☉ star of that size · the objects advance on their own until the first click.

The yardstick of the seminar is compactness: the radius of the object compared with the Schwarzschild radius of its mass, the size at which it would become a black hole. On the left, a zoom in powers of ten: the whole Earth would have to fit in a ball 9 millimetres in radius, a marble; the Sun, in 3 kilometres. That is why relativity is a correction here: on the Earth the surface clock loses 60 microseconds a day, and at the Sun grazing light is deflected by 1.75 arcseconds — the Sobral value. In a white dwarf, r_s/R is 5×10⁻⁴. In a neutron star the radius is only three times r_s: the escape velocity is more than half the speed of light, the redshift 23.5%, a grazing ray is deflected by tens of degrees and we see well over half of the surface. There, no series expansion will save us: the full metric is needed. And the scale carries on to the photon sphere and the horizon.

The angle that matters

From the surface to infinity: ψ(α)

A photon leaves the surface at an angle α to the normal. By the time it reaches the observer, it has turned through ψ around the centre:

b = R sin α√(1 − RS/R)
ψ(b) = ∫01/R du√(b−2 − u²(1 − RSu))exact
1 − cos α ≈ (1 − cos ψ)(1 − RSR)Beloborodov (2002)

Without gravity, ψ = α and the limb is at 90°. With gravity, the limb moves further round: we see behind the star. The approximation is excellent down to R ≈ 3 RS and fails precisely where the secondary image appears.

ψ(α): exact, Beloborodov and flat spacedrag the compactness
For a star, what matters is the relation between the angle α at which the photon leaves the surface and the angle ψ it turns through before reaching us. The exact integral gives ψ for each α. Beloborodov’s approximation, from 2002, is a single line and is excellent for stars that are not too compact — less than one per cent error down to three Schwarzschild radii. But look at the right-hand panel: below about 1.8 R_S the maximum ψ goes past 180 degrees — the whole star becomes visible, and secondary images appear. That is where the approximation stops being useful and the exact integral becomes essential.

Ray tracing backwards

One pixel, one ray

  • Each pixel of the image is a ray that arrives at the observer. Its impact parameter b is the distance to the centre of the image.
  • The geodesic is integrated backwards: the ray escapes to the sky or hits the surface at some point, at an angle α.
  • The pixel takes the colour of the point the light left from — corrected for redshift and for the emission angle.
  • Rays that would never reach the telescope are never computed: that is what makes the method efficient.

With spherical symmetry, ψ depends only on b: one table ψ(b), computed once, serves every pixel.

How it is done in practice. You start from the telescope, not from the star. Each pixel of the image is an incoming ray; the impact parameter is the distance from the pixel to the centre. That ray is integrated backwards: either it escapes, and the pixel is sky, or it hits the surface at some point, with some emission angle. The pixel takes the colour of the point of origin. On the left, the ray of the current pixel seen side-on; on the right, the image building up row by row. In a spherical metric the angle ψ depends only on b, so a table ψ(b) handles every pixel — it is what the labs on my website use, and what the panel on the next slide does in real time.

The most visual effect. Both stars have the same grid of latitudes and longitudes and a marked polar cap. Without gravity, we see half. With gravity, the lines fold out past the limb and we see the other side. Reduce the radius: below 1.76 R_S the whole far pole comes into view, spread into a ring at the edge. A real neutron star has R between 2 and 3 R_S, so 80 to 90% of the surface is always in view — and that is why neutron-star light curves are so much smoother than one might expect.

Pound and Rebka, 1959, in the 22.5-metre tower of the Jefferson laboratory at Harvard. Gravity shifts the frequency of light by gh/c², 2.5 parts in 10¹⁵. To measure it they used the Mössbauer effect of iron-57: a 14.4 keV line narrow enough to see that shift. Light that falls arrives with slightly more energy and is no longer absorbed; to compensate, they moved the absorber away at 0.74 micrometre per second — about 2.7 millimetres per hour. In the middle diagram, on top is the line of the arriving photon, shifted by gravity to +0.74; below, the line the absorber accepts, which slides by Doppler as it moves. When the two match, absorption is maximal — at v = gh/c = 0.74 μm/s. Every 12 seconds source and absorber swap places and the match moves to −0.74, just as they did to cancel systematics. On the right, the same law at a 1.4 solar-mass neutron star: with no exaggeration, 500 nanometres leave green and arrive orange, at 618 nm. It is the same factor neutrinos suffer on leaving the proto-neutron star.

thermal spectrum with an absorption linedrag the compactness
What reaches the telescope. By Liouville’s theorem, I_ν over ν³ is conserved along the ray; in a static field, that makes the observed I equal to g³ times the emitted I at energy E/g. For a black body, the result is another black body, cooler by a factor g. And the radius we infer from the flux is larger, R/g. The dashed curve is the spectrum as it would be seen at the surface; the solid one, what arrives. The absorption line — here a generic line at 0.5 keV at the surface — slides along with it. That is why an identified line measures M/R directly. It is one of the routes, besides the pulse shape, to measuring compactness.

Part I in summary

The ray tracing recipe

  1. Choose the exterior metric: Schwarzschild; Hartle–Thorne or Kerr with rotation; a different one for a different theory of gravity.
  2. Build the table ψ(b) and the delay Δt(b) by integrating the null geodesics once.
  3. For each pixel (or each surface element): b → emitting point, angle α, redshift g, delay.
  4. Intensity: Iobs = g³ I(E/g, α′), with the atmosphere’s beaming and the Doppler shift from rotation.
  5. Add up: image, flux — and, phase by phase, the light curve and the phase-resolved spectrum.

Exact or approximate?

  • Exact integral (or elliptic integrals): always valid, secondary images included.
  • Beloborodov (2002) and Poutanen & Beloborodov (2006): closed formulae for ψ and for the delay, excellent for R ≳ 3 RS.
  • Oblate Schwarzschild (Morsink et al. 2007): a star flattened by rotation, for millisecond pulsars.

This is the engine behind NICER’s mass and radius measurements — and behind the results of Part III.

The recipe, in five steps. The metric; the table of ψ and of the delay, computed once; for each pixel, the point the light came from, the angle, the redshift and the delay; the intensity, with the atmosphere’s beaming and, if the star spins fast, the Doppler shift; and the sum, which gives the image and, phase by phase, the light curve. In practice one chooses between the exact calculation and the closed-form approximations, depending on the compactness and the rotation. This is exactly what lies behind the NICER measurements and the work in Part III.

Cambridge · 28 November 1967

The first pulsar: “LGM-1”

Bell noticed a bit of “scruff” that kept coming back on the radio telescope’s paper chart: pulses every 1.337 s, as regular as clockwork. Nickname: LGM-1, for Little Green Men. It was a spinning neutron star.

Jocelyn Bell, Antony Hewish’s PhD student in Cambridge, was going through kilometres of paper chart from a radio telescope she had helped to build herself. In August 1967 she noticed a little bit of “scruff” that always came back at the same sidereal time. On 28 November she managed to resolve the pulses: one every 1.337 seconds, with clockwork precision. They joked that it was a signal from little green men, LGM-1. Today it is called PSR B1919+21. The 1974 Nobel Prize went to Hewish and Ryle, and not to her; in 2018 she won the Breakthrough Prize and donated the three million dollars to fund scholarships for students under-represented in physics.

The surface in X-rays

Why hot spots appear

Millisecond pulsars

Particles accelerated in the magnetosphere flow back and heat the polar caps to ~10⁶ K. They are NICER’s targets.

Magnetars

Field decay and the currents along twisted field lines heat regions of the crust — which change after each outburst.

Isolated cooling stars

The magnetic field makes heat conduction anisotropic: poles hotter than the equator (the “Magnificent Seven”).

Where the light we are going to trace comes from. In millisecond pulsars, particles accelerated by the field flow back and heat the polar caps: kilometre-sized spots at a million degrees, ideal for NICER. In magnetars, the field itself decays and heats regions of the crust, which change with every burst. And in isolated stars that are simply cooling, the field makes heat conduction anisotropic, and the poles end up hotter. Three telescopes do almost all the work: NICER, for its time resolution; XMM-Newton, for its collecting area; Chandra, for its angular resolution.

Ray tracing a hot spot

One spot, one light curve

static star · exact integral, Beloborodov or flat space
Now the tool in action. A hot spot on a rotating star, seen from far away, drawn by ray tracing pixel by pixel. Three modes: without gravity, with Beloborodov’s approximation and with the exact integral. Without gravity, the spot disappears behind the star and the flux drops to zero. With gravity it hardly ever disappears, and the curve gets smoother: the pulsed fraction drops. Reduce the radius: below 1.76 R_S the secondary image appears, an arc on the other side, and Beloborodov’s approximation starts to go wrong. The shape of the curve depends on the compactness — that is what lets us measure M/R from the light.

Two spots · Beloborodov’s classes

Antipodal, or not

two spots: one at each magnetic pole
With two spots, one at each pole of a centred dipole, Beloborodov’s four classes appear: depending on i and j, we see only the primary; the primary always and the secondary sometimes; one at a time; or both always — and then the flux can become almost constant, a plateau. Now offset the second spot: the dipole is no longer centred and the pulse loses its symmetry. That is exactly what NICER saw in J0030+0451 — and what the analysis of SGR J1745−2900 in Part III called for.

Rotation and atmosphere

Doppler, aberration, delays and beaming

15° spot · 1.4 M☉, 12 km, spinning
  • Doppler: the surface moves at up to 0.1–0.2 c; the approaching side is brighter (bolometric flux ∝ δ⁵).
  • Aberration: the emission angle changes in the frame of the surface.
  • Delays: tens of μs between points on the star — fractions of the period at hundreds of Hz.
  • Beaming: an atmosphere does not emit equally in all directions; the profile depends on it.

Together they make the pulse asymmetric and create harmonics — and the asymmetry measures R separately from M.

What changes when the star spins fast. The surface of a millisecond pulsar moves at 10 or 20% of the speed of light. The side coming towards us is brighter, through the Doppler effect; the emission angles change, through aberration; and the photons from each point arrive with delays that are already fractions of the period. The curve, which was symmetric, becomes lopsided, and harmonics appear. That is good news: the surface speed is 2πR/P, so the asymmetry measures the radius separately from the mass. And the beaming of the atmosphere — black body, hydrogen atmosphere, the pencil beam of a magnetised atmosphere — changes the pulsed fraction. All of this goes into the ray tracing that NICER uses.

Brans & Dicke 1961 · Damour & Esposito-Farèse 1993

What if gravity is not Einstein’s?

In scalar-tensor theories, besides the metric there is a scalar field φ that couples to matter. In the Solar System the effect is tiny; in a neutron star spontaneous scalarisation can occur: the field grows on its own.

Outside the star, the metric is no longer Schwarzschild: it is the Just metric, with a scalar charge Q:

ds² = −(1 − ar)b/adt² + (1 − ar)−b/adr² + (1 − ar)1 − b/ar²dΩ²b = 2GM/c² · a/b = √(1 + Q²) · Einstein frame

Two stars with the same mass and the same radius — “doppelgängers” — bend light in different ways. The light curve can tell the theories apart.

Now gravity. In scalar-tensor theories, gravity has a scalar field in addition to the metric. Brans and Dicke proposed the first one in 1961; Damour and Esposito-Farèse showed in 1993 that, in neutron stars, the field can grow spontaneously — scalarisation — even if in the Solar System everything looks like general relativity. Outside a scalarised star the metric is the Just metric, with a scalar charge Q. The question of our work: do two stars with the same mass and the same radius, one in general relativity and the other scalarised, have different light curves?

Randall & Sundrum 1999 · Shiromizu, Maeda & Sasaki 2000

The Universe on a membrane

In a braneworld, our Universe is a four-dimensional hypersurface — the brane — inside a space with one extra dimension, the bulk. Matter and light are stuck on the brane; only gravity propagates in the bulk.

The idea was put forward to attack the hierarchy problem (why gravity is so weak) and the cosmological-constant problem and accelerated expansion.

Gμν = 8πTμν + κ54Sμν − Eμνeffective Einstein equations on the brane · Shiromizu, Maeda & Sasaki 2000
  • Sμν: local corrections, quadratic in the matter (∝ T²). They matter when the density approaches the brane tension λ.
  • Eμν: the bulk Weyl tensor projected onto the brane — nonlocal: it cannot be computed from brane quantities alone.
  • For λ → ∞ and Eμν → 0, General Relativity is recovered.

In the paper: an effective 4D closure, inspired by the Sahni–Shtanov scenario (2003) — not a complete 5D bulk solution.

What a braneworld is. The idea, from Randall and Sundrum in 1999, is that our four-dimensional Universe is a membrane — the brane — embedded in a space with one more dimension, the bulk. All Standard Model fields, matter and light, are stuck on the brane; only gravity spreads into the bulk. It was proposed to explain why gravity is so much weaker than the other forces and, later, for dark energy. Shiromizu, Maeda and Sasaki showed what Einstein's equations look like from inside the brane: two new terms appear. A local one, quadratic in the matter, which only matters at densities comparable to the brane tension. And a nonlocal one, the projection of the bulk Weyl tensor: the gravitational field of the extra dimension seen from here, which cannot be computed from what lives on the brane alone. In our paper we use an effective, four-dimensional version inspired by the Sahni–Shtanov scenario.

The Weyl sector is the interesting part. The projected Weyl tensor is the gravitational field of the bulk seen from the brane, and on the brane it behaves as an extra fluid — in cosmology it shows up as “dark radiation”. The problem is that it is not determined by the brane equations: a closure has to be chosen. Ours is the simplest one: a Weyl density proportional to the matter density, with coefficient α, and a pressure with equation of state w. On the left of the panel, the fan in α with w = 0: negative α reduces the density that gravitates — a screening — and the star grows; positive α squeezes it. On the right, the fan in w with α = −0.15: w only enters multiplied by α, so different pairs of α and w give similar curves. That degeneracy is what leaves w poorly determined by the data.

Itoh 1970 · Bodmer 1971 · Witten 1984 · the strange matter hypothesis

What if iron is not the bottom of the well?

At extreme densities neutrons may dissolve into quarks. The Bodmer–Witten hypothesis goes further: matter made of u, d and s quarks would be the ground state of matter — with an energy per baryon below that of ⁵⁶Fe, 930 MeV.

Pauli: each flavor has its own Fermi sea. A third flavor opens new levels and the top (μ) drops — even after paying for the s mass.

  • Nuclei don't decay because that would take ~A simultaneous weak d → s conversions.
  • And u–d matter alone must be unstable — otherwise iron itself would have turned into quarks.
  • If it holds, “neutron stars” may be quark stars, bound by the strong force.
the stability window · energy per baryon at p = 0
The strange matter hypothesis of Itoh, Bodmer and Witten. In a nucleus, quarks are locked inside protons and neutrons. But if matter were squeezed until the nucleons dissolved, free u and d quarks could turn some of themselves into s quarks through the weak interaction. Why? Pauli: each flavor has its own Fermi sea. With three flavors, the same number of quarks fits into lower levels and the energy drops — even after paying for the s mass. The hypothesis is that this three-flavor matter has a lower energy per baryon than iron: it would be the ground state of matter. Nuclei do not decay because that would take dozens of simultaneous weak conversions. And u–d matter alone must be unstable, or iron would already have turned into quarks. The turquoise band is the window where uds lies below iron and ud above it: there, quark stars exist. To build stars we need an equation of state. The one by Alford and collaborators starts from QCD at very high density and adds corrections: a potential with three parameters. a4 measures the strong interaction: it is 1 for free quarks and about 0.7 if perturbative QCD is right. a2 combines the strange quark mass and the color-superconducting gap — in the CFL phase, where quarks of different colors and flavors pair up, a2 is m_s² minus four times the gap squared, and it can go negative if the gap is large. Almost every calculation treats a pulsar as a rigid body. But the interior may be superfluid, have a phase transition — a quark core — or gain accreted layers that spin differently, like the Sun's tachocline.

J comes from the derivative of ω̄ at the surface, so it feels the jump: the same star, with the same Ω⁺, has a different moment of inertia.

The other paper is about rotation. Almost every pulsar model assumes the star spins as a rigid body. But the interior may be superfluid and weakly coupled to the crust; there may be a phase transition, with a quark core that, by angular momentum conservation, spins differently; or accreted layers from outside. The Sun has this: the tachocline separates an interior spinning rigidly from an envelope spinning differently. In general relativity, slow rotation is described by Hartle's equation for frame dragging. What the paper does is derive the jump conditions when Ω jumps at a radius R-star: omega-bar jumps with it, its derivative does not. Since the angular momentum J comes from the derivative at the surface, it feels the jump, and the moment of inertia, defined with the observed surface spin, changes. Rigid rotation is an assumption — and it has a cost

  • Near the surface, ΔI/I → ΔΩ/Ω⁺; below ~40% of the radius the effect vanishes. It grows fast for R★ ≳ 0.6 R, and more in light stars.
  • Hybrid stars: the jump sits at the transition to the quark core, and only exceeds 5% with 𝒞 ~ 20% and R★ ≳ 0.8 R (figure).
  • In the radius: with a known mass, ΔR/R = ½ ΔI/I. Ten percent in I is 5% in R — the size of the NICER error bars, which come from ray tracing.
  • Direct measurement of I: the double pulsar J0737−3039A should give I to 11% by 2030 (today, I45 = 1.15–1.48). Jumps of 10–20% in Ω cannot be told from rigid rotation.
  • Energy: with I 10–20% larger, five Fermi gamma-ray pulsars with Lγ > Ė fit back within the rotational energy.
  • I-Love-Q: the quadrupole depends on another function besides J — the universal relations weaken. And in CCOs, accreted layers may spin differently for up to ~10 thousand years.

What this changes. Near the surface, the relative change in the moment of inertia tends to the relative change in rotation, and below about 40% of the radius it vanishes. In the hybrid stars of the figure, the jump sits at the transition to the quark core, which is deep: it only exceeds 5% with 20% jumps in rotation. Now the link with the rest of this talk: if the mass is known and the radius is inferred from the moment of inertia, a 10% error in I becomes 5% in the radius — the size of the NICER error bars, which come from ray tracing. The double pulsar should give the moment of inertia to 11% by 2030, and that will not separate a rigid star from one with 10 to 20% jumps. In the energy budget, an I larger by 10 to 20% lets some gamma-ray pulsars, which seemed to emit more than rotation allows, fit back within it — the table shows three, for 1.45 solar masses; for 2.04 there are two more. And the universal I-Love-Q relations, which assume rigid rotation, weaken.

Conclusions

From geometry to observation — and back

  • In a neutron star, light bending, redshift and delays are not corrections: they define the image and the light curve.
  • Backward ray tracing — with the exact integral when the compactness calls for it — links the metric to what a telescope measures.
  • Applied to Chandra, XMM-Newton and NuSTAR data, it gave the mass, radius, field and spot geometry of magnetars and of an accreting star.
  • Since the light curve depends only on the exterior metric, it also tests gravity: scalar-tensor theories and braneworld.
  • And the interior matters: quark stars and stratified rotation change the M, R and I that ray tracing measures.

Next steps

  • Magnetised atmospheres and exact ray tracing together, for isolated cooling stars.
  • Fast rotation and metrics beyond Schwarzschild in the same fits.
  • Data from the next generation: eXTP, NewAthena, STROBE-X.

The panels in this seminar run in the browser: rafael-lima.pages.dev/talks.

To wrap up. In a neutron star, the relativistic effects on light are not corrections: they are the shape of the signal. Ray tracing, done backwards and with the exact integral when the star is compact, links the geometry to the data. Applied to magnetars and to an accreting star, it measured masses, radii, fields and spots. And, since the light curve depends only on the metric outside, it becomes a test of gravity. The next steps are to combine magnetised atmospheres with exact ray tracing and to get the analyses ready for the next generation of telescopes. Thank you.
