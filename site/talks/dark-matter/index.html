Good afternoon. This seminar is about dark matter inside neutron stars, and it has two halves. In the background, a 1.4 solar-mass neutron star with the SLy equation of state, and particles falling on it along Schwarzschild geodesics: some miss, some enter, scatter, lose energy, and stay. The ones that stay keep colliding and sink into the potential well — the small glowing cloud at the centre. The first part is about how we compute that capture; the second about what that cloud is. The criterion in the corner is the whole story of capture: a particle is captured when its conserved energy, measured at infinity, drops below its rest mass.

The premise

A neutron star is a dark-matter detector

As it crosses the Galaxy, a neutron star sweeps up dark matter: its potential well focuses the particles, its matter stops them.

halo→focusing→collisions→capture→accumulation→regime→heating · collapse · structure

A deep well

Escape speed at the surface √(2GM/R) = 0.59 c for 1.4 M☉ and 11.7 km. Slow halo particles are bent toward the star from a thousand radii away.

A dense target

nb ≈ 5×10³⁸ cm⁻³ at the centre: with σ = 10⁻⁴⁴ cm² a particle meets τ ≈ 6–9 collisions in one crossing.

An old, cold star

What accumulates can heat it, collapse into a black hole, or change its structure — if we know how much is captured and in what state.

The premise. A neutron star has an escape speed of sixty per cent of the speed of light, so slow halo particles, a few hundred kilometres per second, are focused onto it from very far away. Inside, the baryon density is so high that even a cross section of ten to the minus forty-four square centimetres gives several collisions per crossing. The numbers here are for the star I will use throughout: 1.4 solar masses, SLy equation of state, 11.7 kilometres. What is captured accumulates over billions of years and could heat the star, make it collapse, or change its structure. Each of those questions needs two inputs: how much is captured, part one, and in what state it ends up, part two.

1933 – 2006

Matter we only see through gravity

2006, the Bullet Cluster: the lensing mass (blue) is offset from the X-ray gas (pink). Today: about 85% of the matter, a local density ρχ ≈ 0.4 GeV cm⁻³ — and no idea what it is made of.

A short reminder of why we look for it at all. Zwicky, 1933: the galaxies of the Coma cluster move too fast to be held by the mass we see; he called the rest dunkle Materie. Vera Rubin, in the seventies: the rotation curves of spiral galaxies do not fall off where the light ends. And the Bullet Cluster, 2006: after the collision, the lensing mass sits with the galaxies, not with the gas that carries most of the ordinary mass. About eighty-five per cent of the matter in the Universe is of this kind, and near the Sun its density is about 0.4 GeV per cubic centimetre — the number we will use. What it is made of is open, and that is why the particle mass is a free parameter in everything that follows.

The dark-matter background

Two ways to fill the halo

Standard halo · Maxwell–Boltzmann

f(v) ∝ v² e−v²/v0²,  v < vescv₀ = 220 km/s, vesc = 544 km/s

The usual WIMP benchmark: particles of GeV to TeV, a few hundred km/s, isotropic. Used for the Maxwell–Boltzmann results of Part II.

RAR fermions · truncated Fermi–Dirac

f ∝ 1 − e(E − Ec)/kTe(E − μ)/kT + 1,  E ≤ EcRuffini–Argüelles–Rueda

Self-gravitating fermions in general-relativistic equilibrium: a degenerate core at the Galactic centre and a Boltzmann halo. Rotation curves and the S-stars point to ~60–380 keV; we take 100 keV.

Two pictures of the dark matter the star moves through. The standard halo: a Maxwellian with a dispersion of 220 kilometres per second, truncated at the Galactic escape speed — the usual setting for WIMPs, particles of GeV to TeV. And the RAR model, of Ruffini, Argüelles and Rueda: dark matter made of neutral fermions in general-relativistic equilibrium, with a Fermi–Dirac distribution that has an energy cutoff for the particles that escape the Galaxy. That model has a degenerate core at the Galactic centre and a classical halo outside, and fits to the rotation curve and to the orbits of the S-stars point to fermion masses of tens to hundreds of keV. Part one works at 100 keV, which, as we will see, is the hard case for capture.

A short history. Press and Spergel computed capture by the Sun in 1985; Gould built the formalism of the capture rate two years later; Goldman and Nussinov applied it to neutron stars in 1989. Around 2008 to 2010 Kouvaris and Tinyakov turned it into constraints: annihilation heating, and collapse of the captured dark matter into a black hole. In 2017 Baryakhtar and collaborators proposed kinetic heating — the energy deposited by the capture itself. And around 2020 Bell and collaborators put the relativistic kinematics, Pauli blocking, opacity and multiple scattering into one capture treatment. What OBSCURA adds is not a new ingredient in that list; it is to follow individual collision histories through the metric, and use them to test the deterministic approximations of an optical-depth closure.

Captured

after a collision the particle can no longer reach infinity: γ is its Lorentz factor for a static observer at the collision, eν/2 the lapse there.

How the capture rate is built. The star is a static TOV solution, with lapse e to the nu over two inside. The number of particles crossing the surface per unit energy and angular momentum is this simple kernel: eight pi squared m cubed times the distribution function times L, up to the critical angular momentum. Weighting it by the capture probability gives the capture rate. So the whole problem splits into supply, which is geometry, and probability, which is microphysics and transport. OBSCURA computes the kernel P_cap. The capture criterion is a statement about the conserved energy after a collision: once it falls below one, in units of the rest mass, the particle is bound. Note that it is evaluated with the lapse at the collision point — that will matter.

The optical-depth closure

A Poisson count and three frozen choices

Pcap = 1 − e−τ Σk < Nmin τkk!the number of collisions is Poisson with mean τ
τ = 2 ∫rtpR e(ν+λ)/2 Γnχ√(ℰ² − Veff²) drcollisions along the incident geodesic, one crossing

The closure of Sirvente & Rueda (submitted): relativistic and covariant. OBSCURA was built to test it, not to replace it — and reduces to it exactly.

1 · Frozen trajectory

Collisions change the energy, never the path: every particle keeps the incident geodesic.

2 · Deterministic loss

Each collision removes a fixed amount — head-on or the isotropic mean — so capture needs exactly Nmin of them.

3 · Evaluated at the surface

Nmin is computed with the lapse at R, wherever the collision actually happens.

The standard way to close the kernel. Assume the number of collisions along the incident geodesic is Poisson, with mean equal to the optical depth — the collision rate integrated along the orbit, one crossing in and out. Then the particle is captured if it collides at least N_min times, where N_min is the number of energy losses needed to bring its conserved energy below one. The closure we compare with is the relativistic one of Sirvente and Rueda, submitted, and the comparison is in that spirit: we built the Monte Carlo to release three choices they make deterministically. The trajectory is frozen; the loss per collision is a fixed number; and it is evaluated with the lapse of the surface. Each of those can be switched on and off in OBSCURA.

For mχ ≪ mn each collision removes a fraction ~2(mχ/mn)(γ + 1) ≈ 5×10⁻⁴ at most, while it must lose up to a per cent of it: tens of collisions.

Deeper in the star the lapse is smaller and the same collision removes more of ℰ: Nmin halves at the centre.

Slide the mass to the GeV range: one collision suffices — the WIMP limit.

Why 100 keV is the hard case. In an elastic collision on a neutron at rest, the fraction of kinetic energy the neutron takes is given exactly by the formula on the right, with s the invariant mass squared. For a 100 keV particle that is at most five parts in ten thousand. Meanwhile, to be captured the particle must lose about one per cent of its kinetic energy at the surface. So the closure needs N_min of tens — the red and orange staircases: head-on losses and isotropic-mean losses, both with the surface lapse. The dashed curve is the same head-on loss evaluated with the lapse at the centre: the same collision removes more conserved energy where the well is deeper, and N_min halves. When capture is decided by tens of small steps, the distribution of the number of collisions — and where they happen — is the whole story. Move the mass slider up to a GeV: N_min drops to one, and none of this matters.

The transport

One history, collision by collision

  1. Draw a target optical depth −ln U and fly along the interior geodesic with (ℰ, ℒ) conserved, accumulating dτ = nbσ dl (step R/4096).
  2. At the collision, an elastic two-body event on a neutron at rest, isotropic in the centre of mass: energy loss and deflection from the same angles.

Every approximation has a name

GEO-8 interior tetrad transport · COL-3 relativistic event · PAULI-1 blocked rate · STAT-1 Wilson intervals — each documented, cited in the source, and switchable.

Switch off the release, the sampling and the local lapse, and the Monte Carlo is the closure.

What one history is. Draw a target optical depth, minus log of a uniform number, and fly along the interior geodesic until you accumulate it; the production step is R over 4096. At the collision, a relativistic elastic event on a neutron at rest, isotropic in the centre of mass. Energy and direction come from the same pair of angles, so a collision that removes little energy also deflects little — they are correlated as in the physical event. The new Lorentz factor and direction give a new energy and angular momentum, and a new orbit. Then the capture test. Every physical approximation in the code has an identifier, documented in one file and cited in the source where it applies — a discipline we adopted after the code misdescribed its own method once. And there are switches to undo each released ingredient, which is how we recover the closure exactly.

Before measuring differences we must make sure the two calculations agree when they should. Each released ingredient has a switch, and each switch recovers one piece of the closure. With all three off, on the benchmark star at 41 energies and three angular momenta with 5000 histories each, every capture happens at exactly N_min, the number of collisions on the geodesic is Poisson, and the capture probability equals the Poisson tail within 2.8 standard deviations everywhere. The mean count agrees to 0.2 per cent with the column computed by an independent quadrature. The one convention we do not switch is the rate: our unblocked rate treats neutrons at rest, theirs carries Fermi motion through the flux factor; that moves the analytic closure by up to 0.035, and we quantify it separately.

Why does releasing the trajectory lower the capture probability? The histories let us measure it instead of guessing. For a 100 keV particle hitting a neutron, the deflection is large — the light particle bounces off a heavy wall. After the first collision, half of the particles are moving outward; on the frozen chord, almost none. The column left to the surface halves on average, and spreads enormously. A fifth of the released particles never collide again, against three per cent on the frozen chord, while a tail of particles that turn inward collide many more times. So the released trajectory makes the number of collisions more often small and occasionally large: the first lowers capture where few collisions are needed, the second raises it in the tail. The orbit also contracts after each loss, which alone would raise capture; at 100 keV the change of direction wins.

How many collisions · live

A count that is not Poisson

Above the transition almost nothing is captured, so each count is complete: the history ends when the particle leaves. The closure assumes Poisson with the mean τ. The mean is right; the shape is not.

PNB(k; r, p),  r = m²v − m, p = mvPoisson count with a Gamma-distributed τ

In the paper: variance 8–13 times the mean. This is a random walk out of an optically thick sphere, not a straight chord.

The Monte Carlo does more than move the capture probability: it measures the whole distribution of the number of collisions. At energies above the transition almost nothing is captured, so the count of each history is complete. Live again, on our SLy star: the mean is close to the optical depth of the incident geodesic, as the closure assumes. The distribution is not. The variance is many times the mean — around nine here; between 8 and 13 in the paper's runs — while a Poisson law has variance equal to the mean. Physically, a light particle in a dense star performs a random walk out of an optically thick sphere: many leave quickly, some wander for a long time. The negative binomial, a Poisson whose mean itself fluctuates, follows the histogram much better.

Can we keep a closed form, but with the right count law? We calibrate three laws on independent runs above the transition, where the counts are complete, and test them inside the transition, where capture happens; calibration and test share no histories. Poisson with the measured mean misses by 0.15 to 0.19 on average. The negative binomial roughly halves that. A zero-and-one-inflated negative binomial, which treats separately the particles that collide zero or one time, gets to four to six hundredths. But I want to be honest about it: the residuals are systematic, far larger than the statistical and calibration errors, and the laws were tested at fixed mass, star and cross section. They are better closures, not a universal law.

A contrast, not a second decomposition. On the maximum-mass SLy star the closure has an optical depth three times larger, and its curves are steep steps. The transport begins to lose particles already at low energy and keeps more than ten per cent of them at energies twenty times higher, where both closures give zero. The mean absolute difference is three tenths. So the broadening is present in both stars, and it is larger where the star is denser — exactly the stars where capture matters most.

Pauli blocking and cooling

Pauli blocking · implemented, not yet in the campaigns

The recoiling neutron must leave the Fermi sea: the blocked optical depth is 7×10⁻⁵ of the unblocked one.

Two things beyond the controlled benchmark. Pauli blocking is implemented: the recoiling neutron must end outside the Fermi sea, and that suppresses the optical depth by almost five orders of magnitude. But the few collisions that are allowed remove more than half of the kinetic energy each. The lesson is methodological: you cannot multiply the unblocked optical depth by a suppression factor and keep the old N_min — both must come from the same blocked kernel. Those campaigns are next. On the right, what happens after capture: we keep following captured particles until their local kinetic energy drops below three halves kT. Capture costs five collisions; cooling costs thousands, with hundreds of excursions out of the star — and takes only seconds, almost all of it outside. This is a cooling time in a model with no heating, not thermalisation. And that brings us to the question of part two.

A word about the code itself, because a Monte Carlo is only as good as its checks. Each stage is compared with something independent: the interior transport against a quadrature of the same orbit integrals that shares no code with it; the capture probability under step refinement; the exterior geodesics against the analytic turning point; the focusing against its known low-speed limit; the incidence sampler against isotropy; the CUDA backend against the CPU one; the population integrator against the analytic solution; and the Fermi equation of state against its two limits. Two of the three defects we found in the geodesic tracer were invisible to reasoning and only appeared against an independent method — that is the rule we follow now.

Part I · in short

What the closure misses

  • The Monte Carlo reduces exactly to the Poisson closure when its released ingredients are switched off.
  • Where the loss is evaluated and whether the trajectory is released move Pcap by ±0.1 each, in opposite directions: right on average, 0.16–0.18 RMS off in shape.
  • Collision counts are strongly overdispersed; negative-binomial laws calibrated out of sample do much better than Poisson.
  • Pauli blocking changes rate and kernel together: it must be measured, not rescaled.
A correct mean number of collisions is not enough for a Poisson closure.
the one-line summary of Part I
Part one in four lines. The transport is a controlled extension of the closure: it reduces to it exactly. Two deterministic choices — where the loss is evaluated, and the frozen trajectory — each move the capture probability by about a tenth, in opposite directions, so the closure is right on average and wrong in shape. The count of collisions is overdispersed, and better count laws exist, with honest residuals. And blocking has to be done consistently. Now, the particles are captured: what are they?

The question

A classical tracer

The usual assumption: the particles thermalise into a Boltzmann cloud in the fixed potential of the star. Right for a dilute gas.

n ∝ e−mΔΦ/kT

A degenerate tracer

Fermions in a deep well can reach a phase-space density where Pauli matters — while still too light to change the gravity.

EF ≳ kT

A dark component

Dark-matter admixed neutron stars: the dark fluid takes part in hydrostatic balance and shifts the mass–radius relation.

two-fluid TOV
Capture calculations give a particle budget. Models of dark-matter admixed neutron stars ask how a macroscopic dark fluid changes the structure. In between there is a question usually skipped: what regime does the captured population occupy? The standard assumption is the left one — a dilute classical gas in thermal equilibrium, a Boltzmann cloud in the potential of the star. But the same deep potential that confines the particles can push their phase-space density to where Pauli exclusion matters, the middle case, even if their mass is negligible. Only when the dark mass becomes comparable to the baryonic mass where it sits do we need the two-fluid structure on the right.

There is no general reason for the two sets of thresholds to coincide.

The analysis rests on two numbers. S compares the dark mass with the baryonic mass enclosed in the same central region, the radius that holds ninety per cent of the particles: it asks whether the dark matter contributes to the local gravitational field. Since S is the ratio of the two local accelerations, the thresholds have a direct meaning: below ten to the minus three, neglecting the dark matter's gravity is a per-mille error. Theta compares the thermal energy with the Fermi energy of the captured particles: it asks whether Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics are adequate. Large Theta, classical; small Theta, degenerate. Note the convention: Theta is the inverse of the usual degeneracy parameter. The thresholds are operational, not new constants. The point of separating them is that they answer different questions — and there is no reason for them to be crossed together.

The two descriptions being compared. On the left, the classical reference: particles in thermal equilibrium at the core temperature, a Boltzmann cloud in the fixed potential of the star — the baryons make the potential, the dark matter does not feed back. Full thermal profile in the SLy star, as in the previous panel.

Why should degeneracy come first? A back-of-the-envelope that I find useful, not in the paper in this form. A cloud much smaller than the star sits in the harmonic core of the potential. Its radius scales as the square root of T over m, but it cancels between S and the central density: the central density is about 4.6 times S times rho_c over m, whatever the temperature. Put that in the non-relativistic Fermi energy and Theta scales as T times m to the five thirds times S to the minus two thirds. So the value of S at which the gas becomes degenerate grows as m to the five halves. The table is from the full calculation of the previous panel: for a GeV particle at ten to the five kelvin, ten to the minus eleven; for a keV fermion, ten to the minus twenty-four. Only around a TeV does the degeneracy threshold approach the self-gravity one.

The pipeline

From the star to the diagnostics

baryonic TOV star→bmax(v)→Γinc→fcap (Monte Carlo)→Cχ→Nχ(t)→ρχ(r)→(S, Θ)
Γinc = nχπ ∫ bmax²v f(v) dvfocused flux onto the surface
Cχ = Γinc fcapfcap: captured over sampled
dNχdt = Cχ − CANχ² − CENχannihilation, evaporation: off in the fiducial

The order matters: capture supplies the particle budget, S tests whether the fixed gravitational background is adequate, Θ tests whether the classical closure is.

The pipeline of the paper, end to end. The TOV star gives the background. The exterior geodesics give b_max as a function of speed, and averaging over the halo gives the focused incidence rate. The Monte Carlo gives the capture fraction, and the product is the capture rate. The population equation integrates it in time; with annihilation and evaporation switched off it is just C times t. That number normalises the profile, and from the profile come S and Theta. The order is the logic of the paper: first the budget, then gravity, then statistics.

The mass trend

Heavier particles are less degenerate: d log Θc/d log mχ ≈ 0.49 (MB) and 0.42 (RAR–FD). The degenerate quadrants hold 85–89% of the lightest models and 21–28% of the heaviest.

Not a change of particle

III → IV is the same Fermi gas becoming heavier where it sits: the growth of S, not of the particle mass.

MB vs RAR–FD: corr(log S, log Θc) = −0.652 vs −0.154.

Two regularities. The straight families are not a new parameter: they are the two limits of the Fermi energy. Along a track where only the ambient density changes, the captured number changes, the profile shape does not, so S is proportional to the central density, and the Fermi momentum to its cube root. Non-relativistic Fermi energy gives Theta proportional to S to the minus two thirds; relativistic, to the minus one third. The fits in the Fermi–Dirac survey give exactly minus 0.667 and minus 0.334 — the panel on the previous slide reproduces these counts from the data. Second, the mass trend: heavier particles are more classical. And moving from quadrant three to four is not a different particle; it is the same Fermi gas becoming gravitationally important.

What comes next

The right model for each quadrant

I

S ≪ 1, Θ ≫ 1

a Boltzmann cloud in the fixed star — the standard assumption, justified

II

S ≳ 0.1, Θ ≫ 1

classical, but it shapes its own potential

III

S ≪ 1, Θ ≲ 1

a capture-normalised Fermi tracer in the fixed star — finite-T if Θ ~ 1–10

IV

S ≳ 0.1, Θ ≪ 1

only here: a two-fluid TOV, the dark-matter admixed star

Capture calculations and admixed-star models are not interchangeable endpoints. A small Mχ/M★ or S says the structure is unchanged; it does not justify a Boltzmann profile.

Not yet included

Annihilation heating, collapse and black-hole formation, rotation, magnetic fields, and a self-consistent time evolution of the captured Fermi gas. Θ is a diagnostic, not a finite-temperature solution.

What the map buys us is a decision rule for the next model. In quadrant one, the usual Boltzmann tracer is fine. In quadrant three — small S, small or order-one Theta — the next description is a Fermi tracer normalised by capture, in the fixed star, at finite temperature when Theta is between one and ten, which is where the fiducial sits. Only when S grows toward the transitional band does one need the two-fluid TOV of dark-matter admixed stars. The calculation is diagnostic: no annihilation heating, no collapse, no rotation, no self-consistent evolution of the Fermi gas. Those are the extensions.

Conclusions

Capture, then character

  • OBSCURA follows dark-matter collision histories through the interior metric and reduces exactly to the optical-depth closure.
  • At 100 keV, the frozen trajectory and the surface evaluation each move Pcap by ~0.1, in opposite directions; the count of collisions is overdispersed.
  • After capture, S and Θ answer different questions: the fiducial population is an extreme tracer (S ~ 10⁻¹⁶) in the transitional band (Θc ≈ 3–5).
  • For light particles degeneracy comes before self-gravity: the thermalised-WIMP assumption has to be tested in (S, Θ), not by Mχ/M★.

Next steps

  • Pauli-blocked capture campaigns with the consistent blocked kernel.
  • Capture rates over the full incident phase space, RAR halo included.
  • Thermalisation with heating and detailed balance, then a finite-T Fermi tracer.
  • Two-fluid structure once S leaves the tracer regime.

The panels in this seminar run in the browser, on the same physics as OBSCURA: rafael-lima.pages.dev/talks.

To wrap up. OBSCURA follows each collision through the metric and reduces to the standard closure when it should; with it we measured what the closure misses at 100 keV — two opposite effects of a tenth each, and a strongly overdispersed count of collisions. Downstream, the captured population has to be characterised by two independent numbers. The fiducial one weighs nothing and is not classical; across the survey, light particles become degenerate long before they matter gravitationally. The next steps are the blocked campaigns, the full capture rates with the RAR halo, a real thermalisation, and the two-fluid structure where it is needed. Thank you.
