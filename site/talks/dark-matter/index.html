Good afternoon. This seminar is about dark matter inside neutron stars, and it has three parts. In the background, a 1.4 solar-mass neutron star with the SLy equation of state, and particles falling on it along Schwarzschild geodesics: some miss, some enter, scatter, lose energy, and stay. The ones that stay keep colliding and sink into the potential well — the small glowing cloud at the centre. The first part is about dark matter itself: why we believe in it, what it could be, and where it has been searched for. The second is about how we compute that capture; the third about what that cloud is. The criterion in the corner is the whole story of capture: a particle is captured when its conserved energy, measured at infinity, drops below its rest mass.

What survived

Today's H₀ is seven times smaller than his, and X-rays later found hot gas outweighing the galaxies. The excess shrinks and stays: baryons are about one sixth of a cluster.

Zwicky, 1933. He had radial velocities for only seven galaxies in Coma, and they scatter by a thousand kilometres per second or more. Take 800 galaxies of a billion solar masses in a sphere of ten to the twenty-four centimetres: the virial theorem gives 80 kilometres per second. That 80 is a three-dimensional speed and a Doppler shift measures one component, about 46, so the ratio squared is close to 470, which is how he reaches “at least 400 times”. He called what was missing dunkle Materie. Two corrections since then. He used Hubble's expansion rate, about 500 kilometres per second per megaparsec, so distances were too small and the mass-to-light ratio was overestimated by about seven. And in the seventies X-ray satellites found hot gas with more mass than all the galaxies. Neither removes the problem: baryons add up to about one sixth of a cluster's mass, and with more than a thousand velocities today Coma's dispersion is very close to his. He was not the first to use the words: Poincaré wrote “matière obscure” in 1906, and Kapteyn and Oort looked for it near the Sun. But then the subject sat quiet for forty years.

Forty years later the problem came back inside single galaxies. From 1970 Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured optical rotation curves, first in Andromeda and then in dozens of spirals, and at the same time Albert Bosma and others followed neutral hydrogen at 21 centimetres far beyond the stars. In 1973 Ostriker and Peebles had already argued that thin discs need a massive halo to be stable. If the mass stopped where the light stops, speed would fall as one over the square root of r. It does not. This panel is one modern galaxy, NGC 3198, in the SPARC sample: the dots are measured, and the amber curve is what the observed stars and gas predict. With light only, even the best mass-to-light ratio, 1.3, about three times what stellar populations allow, leaves a reduced chi-squared of 235. Add an NFW halo and it drops to 1.4, at a mass-to-light ratio of 0.45, with about four and a half times more dark than visible mass inside the last point. Drag the sliders. Lensing changes the argument, because it measures mass without assuming anything about orbits. The Bullet Cluster is two clusters that passed through each other. The hot gas, in pink, carries most of the ordinary mass; it collided and was left behind. The galaxies and the lensing mass, in blue, went straight through. Clowe and collaborators found the centre of the total mass offset from the centre of the baryons at eight sigma, and argued that no change in the force law can do that, since gravity should point to where the baryons are. Harvey and collaborators then stacked 72 cluster collisions and got a limit on the self-interaction, 0.47 square centimetres per gram. Hold on to the word “barely”: rare collisions are the regime in which a neutron star becomes interesting.

The strongest evidence does not come from galaxies at all. Before recombination, photons and baryons oscillate as one fluid in the potential wells of the primordial perturbations, and the spectrum of the cosmic microwave background is a frozen record of those oscillations. The dots are Planck. The curve is a real Boltzmann calculation with CLASS, on a grid I ran for this slide; the only thing changing is what the sliders say. Baryons add inertia to the fluid, so compressions go deeper than rarefactions: odd peaks over even peaks. Dark matter does not oscillate, so its potential wells survive radiation pressure, and that is why the height of the third peak relative to the first counts it. Now take the dark matter away. I keep the acoustic angle fixed, measured to 0.03 per cent, so that the peaks stay roughly in place, and the price is in the readout: without dark matter you need a Hubble constant of about 240 and a Universe of 9.4 billion years. The wells decay as the modes enter the horizon, which drives the oscillations harder: the first peak more than doubles, the third hardly moves, and it ends at a fifth of the first instead of almost a half. Chi-squared per point goes from one to over two thousand. That chi-squared uses the diagonal error bars only, so it is a guide for the eye, not the Planck likelihood. Planck gets the dark-matter density to under one per cent, about five times the baryons.

Could it simply be ordinary matter we cannot see? Two independent measurements say no. Deuterium made in the first three minutes fixes the baryon density, and the CMB measures the same number 380 thousand years later; Cooke and collaborators find agreement within two sigma. That total is five times too small. And there is a dynamical argument, in this plot, again from CLASS, at a wavenumber of half an inverse megaparsec, with amplitudes normalised to the Planck primordial spectrum. Dark matter starts growing at equality, in violet. Baryons are locked to the photons, oscillate, and only at recombination do they fall into the dark wells and catch up, in amber. Remove the dark matter and keep the same primordial fluctuations, the red curve: the baryons are erased by photon diffusion and end at one part in a thousand today, a factor of a thousand below the threshold for collapse. There would be no galaxies. The budget: dark energy, 68 and a half per cent; dark matter, 26 and a half; atoms, about 5. Neutrinos do exist, and they are dark, but the mass bound makes them about one per cent at most, and they would be hot. So dark matter is physics beyond the Standard Model.

The serious alternative is that we have the wrong gravity. Milgrom proposed in 1983 that below an acceleration of about ten to the minus ten metres per second squared, dynamics changes. That gives flat rotation curves and the Tully–Fisher relation at once, and it should be taken seriously: McGaugh, Lelli and Schombert showed 2693 points in 153 galaxies following one relation, with baryons predicting the total acceleration even where dark matter dominates. Any dark-matter theory has to explain why. But gravity is universal, so go through the scales. Wide binaries from Gaia are a clean test and they are disputed: Banik and collaborators prefer Newton at 19 sigma, Chae finds an anomaly. Clusters remain short of mass by about a factor of two even in MOND, and the Bullet Cluster's potential follows the galaxies. The relativistic versions built to emulate dark matter, in which matter and gravitational waves follow different metrics, were ruled out by GW170817, a neutron-star merger, as it happens. The newest theory, AeST by Skordis and Złośnik, does fit the CMB and the matter power spectrum, but through a new field that behaves like dust, which is hard to tell from dark matter. So most of the field works with particles, and that is what I will do too.

So what do we actually know? It is not baryons, it moves slowly, it does not couple to light, it has lasted for the age of the Universe, it barely collides, and we know its cosmic density to one per cent; near the Sun it is about 0.4 GeV per cubic centimetre, the number we will use. Gravity tells us the total mass, and nothing about how it is divided into particles. So the allowed masses run over some ninety orders of magnitude. At the light end, waves: ultralight scalars, where the Lyman-alpha forest now gives two times ten to the minus twenty electronvolts, which strongly disfavours the original fuzzy dark matter; axion-like particles; and the QCD axion. In the middle, particles. Fermions cannot go below about a tenth of a keV, because Pauli would not let them fit in dwarf galaxies. Then sterile neutrinos at keV, where warm dark matter limits are tight; RAR fermions, the case we use later, tens to hundreds of keV; sub-GeV dark sectors; WIMPs. At the heavy end, objects: primordial black holes. OGLE microlensing rules them out as the dominant component from ten to the minus eight to a thousand solar masses, which leaves the asteroid-mass window open. The three bright bands are where most of the effort has gone, and two of them come next.

The idea that dominated for forty years. Suppose a new particle was in thermal equilibrium in the hot early Universe. When the temperature falls below its mass, its abundance should drop exponentially, but annihilation needs two particles to meet, and once the expansion outruns that rate the comoving number freezes. The second most explored idea has a pedigree the WIMP does not: nobody invented it for dark matter. QCD allows a term that violates CP, with an angle theta-bar that could be anything from zero to two pi. The neutron electric dipole moment says it is below ten to the minus ten. Peccei and Quinn explained that by turning the angle into a dynamical field that relaxes to zero; Weinberg and Wilczek noticed that field has a particle, the axion. Its mass is set by a single scale f_a. And it comes with a production mechanism: the field starts displaced and oscillates from the QCD epoch on, which behaves exactly like cold dark matter. Sikivie then showed how to find it. In a strong magnetic field an axion converts into a microwave photon of energy equal to its mass, and a tuned cavity can collect it, one frequency at a time. Because the mass is unknown, you scan. The plot shows what has been excluded, in amber. The two lines are the benchmark couplings. ADMX and CAPP reach the lower one, DFSZ, around 3 to 5 micro-electronvolts, and everywhere else the searches are still above it. That reach is about a tenth of the logarithmic range scanned from 2 to 120. Sensitivity is finally there, but the search is slow, and it is one of the most promising places for a discovery in the next decade. Neutron stars join this game too, through axion–photon conversion in their magnetospheres, but that is another talk.

One candidate deserves more time, because it is the one we use, and because it is a piece of relativistic astrophysics more than of particle physics. Forget new forces for a moment. Take neutral fermions, gravity and the Pauli principle, and solve Einstein's equations at finite temperature. The idea has a long pedigree. Oppenheimer and Volkoff in 1939: Pauli pressure against gravity gives a limit mass. Ruffini and Bonazzola in 1969 developed self-gravitating quantum particles in general relativity. In the eighties and early nineties Ruffini, Stella, Merafina, Ingrosso and collaborators added semi-degenerate fermions and an energy cutoff. Bilić and collaborators in 2002 put a Newtonian fermion ball at the Galactic Centre. The model that carries the initials is Ruffini, Argüelles and Rueda, 2015; Jorge Rueda, the R, is my collaborator in the rest of this talk. The extended version of 2018 brought the cutoff back and made the core compact enough to sit inside the orbit of the star S2. Three things set it apart: one particle and one distribution from the core to the edge of the Galaxy; no new force needed; and, unlike cold dark matter, the particle mass is visible in the structure.

The ingredients. The distribution is Fermi–Dirac, with one change: it goes to zero at a cutoff energy, because a particle with more energy than that has left the Galaxy. So this is a fermionic King model, and in the limit of no cutoff you recover plain Fermi–Dirac. It is not an arbitrary choice: it is a stationary state of a generalised Fokker–Planck equation that includes violent relaxation and evaporation, and a maximum of a coarse-grained entropy. From it you get density and pressure at each radius, and the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equations close the system. Equilibrium in general relativity then fixes how things vary with the metric. Tolman: temperature is higher where the potential is deeper. Klein: the same for the chemical potential. And energy conservation along a geodesic says the cutoff is the same escape energy seen from everywhere. Those three become one simple statement in dimensionless variables: beta, the temperature; theta, the degeneracy; and W, the cutoff. W and theta differ by a constant. So there are only four numbers: the particle mass and the three central values. Everything else is a solution.

Here is why this model belongs in a talk about neutron stars. The core is a neutron star, rescaled. It is the same equation: Pauli pressure against gravity in general relativity. The Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit goes as the Planck mass cubed over the fermion mass squared, so one formula gives every case. Put in the neutron and you get the classic result of 1939, 0.71 solar masses and 9.5 kilometres, for an ideal gas; real neutron stars go higher because of nuclear forces. Put in 100 keV and you get 63 million solar masses, in a radius of 5.6 astronomical units. Sizes and masses go as one over m squared, density as m to the fourth. So a core at the edge of collapse has a central density of 0.46 grams per cubic centimetre: less dense than water. The dimensionless compactness is the same, 4.5 Schwarzschild radii. I checked these with a cold TOV integration, and they match the published 6.27 times ten to the seven. On the plot, the white line is the limit: above it, no equilibrium. The amber line is Sgr A*. And above the limit a core collapses, which would give a black hole seed of tens of millions of solar masses with no star involved. That is a prediction, and an interesting one in the JWST era.

The premise

A neutron star is a dark-matter detector

As it crosses the Galaxy, a neutron star sweeps up dark matter: its potential well focuses the particles, its matter stops them.

A deep well

Escape speed at the surface √(2GM/R) = 0.59 c for 1.4 M☉ and 11.7 km. Slow halo particles are bent toward the star from a thousand radii away.

A dense target

nb ≈ 5×10³⁸ cm⁻³ at the centre: with σ = 10⁻⁴⁴ cm² a particle meets τ ≈ 6–9 collisions in one crossing.

An old, cold star

What accumulates can heat it, collapse into a black hole, or change its structure — if we know how much is captured and in what state.

So here is the premise of the rest of the talk. Laboratory experiments lose the light particles because a slow, light particle cannot move a heavy nucleus. A neutron star has neither problem. Its escape speed is sixty per cent of the speed of light, so slow halo particles, a few hundred kilometres per second, are focused onto it from very far away and arrive fast. Inside, the baryon density is so high that even a cross section of ten to the minus forty-four square centimetres gives several collisions per crossing. For a 100 keV particle a collision on a neutron there transfers some ten electronvolts, against a micro-electronvolt on xenon on Earth, and the exposure is billions of years. The numbers here are for the star I will use throughout: 1.4 solar masses, SLy equation of state, 11.7 kilometres. What is captured accumulates and could heat the star, make it collapse, or change its structure. Each of those questions needs two inputs: how much is captured, part two, and in what state it ends up, part three.

A short history. Press and Spergel computed capture by the Sun in 1985; Gould built the formalism of the capture rate two years later; Goldman and Nussinov applied it to neutron stars in 1989. Around 2008 to 2010 Kouvaris and Tinyakov turned it into constraints: annihilation heating, and collapse of the captured dark matter into a black hole. In 2017 Baryakhtar and collaborators proposed kinetic heating — the energy deposited by the capture itself. And around 2020 Bell and collaborators put the relativistic kinematics, Pauli blocking, opacity and multiple scattering into one capture treatment. What OBSCURA adds is not a new ingredient in that list; it is to follow individual collision histories through the metric, and use them to test the deterministic approximations of an optical-depth closure.

A word about the code itself, because a Monte Carlo is only as good as its checks. Each stage is compared with something independent: the interior transport against a quadrature of the same orbit integrals that shares no code with it; the capture probability under step refinement; the exterior geodesics against the analytic turning point; the focusing against its known low-speed limit; the incidence sampler against isotropy; the CUDA backend against the CPU one; the population integrator against the analytic solution; and the Fermi equation of state against its two limits. Two of the three defects we found in the geodesic tracer were invisible to reasoning and only appeared against an independent method — that is the rule we follow now.

Part II · in short

What the closure misses

  • The Monte Carlo reduces exactly to the Poisson closure when its released ingredients are switched off.
  • Where the loss is evaluated and whether the trajectory is released move Pcap by ±0.1 each, in opposite directions: right on average, 0.16–0.18 RMS off in shape.
  • Collision counts are strongly overdispersed; negative-binomial laws calibrated out of sample do much better than Poisson.
  • Pauli blocking changes rate and kernel together: it must be measured, not rescaled.
A correct mean number of collisions is not enough for a Poisson closure.
the one-line summary of Part II
Part two in four lines. The transport is a controlled extension of the closure: it reduces to it exactly. Two deterministic choices — where the loss is evaluated, and the frozen trajectory — each move the capture probability by about a tenth, in opposite directions, so the closure is right on average and wrong in shape. The count of collisions is overdispersed, and better count laws exist, with honest residuals. And blocking has to be done consistently. Now, the particles are captured: what are they?

A dark component

Dark-matter admixed neutron stars: the dark fluid takes part in hydrostatic balance and shifts the mass–radius relation.

two-fluid TOV
Capture calculations give a particle budget. Models of dark-matter admixed neutron stars ask how a macroscopic dark fluid changes the structure. In between there is a question usually skipped: what regime does the captured population occupy? The standard assumption is the left one — a dilute classical gas in thermal equilibrium, a Boltzmann cloud in the potential of the star. But the same deep potential that confines the particles can push their phase-space density to where Pauli exclusion matters, the middle case, even if their mass is negligible. Only when the dark mass becomes comparable to the baryonic mass where it sits do we need the two-fluid structure on the right.

There is no general reason for the two sets of thresholds to coincide.

The analysis rests on two numbers. S compares the dark mass with the baryonic mass enclosed in the same central region, the radius that holds ninety per cent of the particles: it asks whether the dark matter contributes to the local gravitational field. Since S is the ratio of the two local accelerations, the thresholds have a direct meaning: below ten to the minus three, neglecting the dark matter's gravity is a per-mille error. Theta compares the thermal energy with the Fermi energy of the captured particles: it asks whether Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics are adequate. Large Theta, classical; small Theta, degenerate. Note the convention: Theta is the inverse of the usual degeneracy parameter. The thresholds are operational, not new constants. The point of separating them is that they answer different questions — and there is no reason for them to be crossed together.

Why should degeneracy come first? A back-of-the-envelope that I find useful, not in the paper in this form. A cloud much smaller than the star sits in the harmonic core of the potential. Its radius scales as the square root of T over m, but it cancels between S and the central density: the central density is about 4.6 times S times rho_c over m, whatever the temperature. Put that in the non-relativistic Fermi energy and Theta scales as T times m to the five thirds times S to the minus two thirds. So the value of S at which the gas becomes degenerate grows as m to the five halves. Two regularities. The straight families are not a new parameter: they are the two limits of the Fermi energy. Along a track where only the ambient density changes, the captured number changes, the profile shape does not, so S is proportional to the central density, and the Fermi momentum to its cube root. Non-relativistic Fermi energy gives Theta proportional to S to the minus two thirds; relativistic, to the minus one third. The fits in the Fermi–Dirac survey give exactly minus 0.667 and minus 0.334 — the panel on the previous slide reproduces these counts from the data. Second, the mass trend: heavier particles are more classical. And moving from quadrant three to four is not a different particle; it is the same Fermi gas becoming gravitationally important.

Not yet included

Annihilation heating, collapse and black-hole formation, rotation, magnetic fields, and a self-consistent time evolution of the captured Fermi gas. Θ is a diagnostic, not a finite-temperature solution.

What the map buys us is a decision rule for the next model. In quadrant one, the usual Boltzmann tracer is fine. In quadrant three — small S, small or order-one Theta — the next description is a Fermi tracer normalised by capture, in the fixed star, at finite temperature when Theta is between one and ten, which is where the fiducial sits. Only when S grows toward the transitional band does one need the two-fluid TOV of dark-matter admixed stars. The calculation is diagnostic: no annihilation heating, no collapse, no rotation, no self-consistent evolution of the Fermi gas. Those are the extensions.

Conclusions

Capture, then character

  • OBSCURA follows dark-matter collision histories through the interior metric and reduces exactly to the optical-depth closure.
  • At 100 keV, the frozen trajectory and the surface evaluation each move Pcap by ~0.1, in opposite directions; the count of collisions is overdispersed.
  • After capture, S and Θ answer different questions: the fiducial population is an extreme tracer (S ~ 10⁻¹⁶) in the transitional band (Θc ≈ 3–5).
  • For light particles degeneracy comes before self-gravity: the thermalised-WIMP assumption has to be tested in (S, Θ), not by Mχ/M★.

Next steps

  • Pauli-blocked capture campaigns with the consistent blocked kernel.
  • Capture rates over the full incident phase space, RAR halo included.
  • Thermalisation with heating and detailed balance, then a finite-T Fermi tracer.
  • Two-fluid structure once S leaves the tracer regime.

To wrap up. OBSCURA follows each collision through the metric and reduces to the standard closure when it should; with it we measured what the closure misses at 100 keV — two opposite effects of a tenth each, and a strongly overdispersed count of collisions. Downstream, the captured population has to be characterised by two independent numbers. The fiducial one weighs nothing and is not classical; across the survey, light particles become degenerate long before they matter gravitationally. The next steps are the blocked campaigns, the full capture rates with the RAR halo, a real thermalisation, and the two-fluid structure where it is needed. Thank you.
